# Thinning Spots: Causes and How to Fix Them

**Date:** 2026-11-14. Research summary, not medical advice: see a dermatologist for a diagnosis.

## 1. Bottom line

*This is a research summary, not medical advice. Only a clinician who examines you can diagnose the cause.*

- **The cause is most likely common and not dangerous.** Pattern hair loss is the most common cause, and about half of people show it by age 50. The next most common is shedding that starts 2 to 6 months after a trigger such as illness, childbirth, a crash diet or a new medicine. With both, the scalp usually looks normal and does not hurt ([DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/hair-loss), [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/shedding)).
- **This week:**
  - Look at a shed hair. A white bulb at one end means it came out from the root, and no bulb means it broke off.
  - Zoom in on the thin spot with your phone to check that you can still see pores.
  - Take baseline photos of your part, crown and hairline in the same light.
  - Write down anything that happened in the 2 to 6 months before the loss started.
  - Loosen tight styles.
  - Don't stop a prescribed medicine on your own ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/shedding)).
- **This month:**
  - If it is shedding, keep a diary. It usually settles over 6 to 9 months ([DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/telogen-effluvium)).
  - See a GP for targeted blood tests (thyroid, iron) if you also feel tired, cold or hot, or have weight change or heavy periods ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hair-loss/)).
  - For pattern loss, ask a doctor before you start treatment. The over-the-counter minoxidil label says not to treat yourself if no one in your family has hair loss, or if the loss is sudden or patchy.
- **Treatments with the strongest evidence:**
  - **For pattern loss:** topical minoxidil, 2% or 5% ([Cochrane 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27225981/)). For men only, finasteride 1 mg ([Propecia label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/drugInfo.cfm?setid=6f904709-65aa-44ce-b144-b4c8a0416e36)).
  - **For severe alopecia areata:** JAK-inhibitor tablets such as baricitinib, ritlecitinib and deuruxolitinib. These carry serious boxed warnings ([Cochrane 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37870096/)).
  - **For small patches of alopecia areata:** steroid injections are standard care ([BAD](https://www.bad.org.uk/pils/alopecia-areata/)).
  - **How long it takes:** allow about 6 months before judging a treatment. The benefit lasts only while you keep using it.
- **Next tier (moderate evidence):** low-dose oral minoxidil (off-label), dutasteride for men, laser caps or combs, and microneedling added to another treatment ([Sobral 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39425514/), [Gupta 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35107565/), [Perez 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39404126/), [Pei 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38239003/)).
- **Don't waste money on these:**
  - **Supplements:** biotin or "hair supplements" if you are not deficient, and mega-doses. Too much selenium, vitamin A or vitamin E can itself cause hair loss, and biotin can distort blood tests ([Patel 2017](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=28879195), [AAD tips](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/tips)).
  - **Home remedies:** rosemary oil, caffeine shampoo, pumpkin seed oil, onion juice and scalp massage. All have weak evidence or none ([Canadian consensus 2025](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40986632)).
  - **PRP injections:** the evidence is low certainty ([Cruciani 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34967722/)).
  - **Laser devices:** "FDA-cleared" does not mean proven to work.
- **Camouflage is fine while you wait.** Fibres, sprays, toppers and wigs can help how you feel, but they do not regrow hair. Don't let them delay a diagnosis ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/tips)).
- **See a dermatologist promptly if you notice any of these.** Early treatment can stop scarring spreading, and hair lost to scarring cannot regrow ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/diagnosis-treat), [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/tinea-capitis)).
  - Pain, burning, itching, redness, pus or scale where the hair is thinning.
  - Smooth, shiny skin with no pores.
  - A crown patch that keeps getting bigger.
  - A hairline moving back in a band, with eyebrow loss.
  - Coin-sized bald patches that appear quickly or spread, or loss of eyebrows or eyelashes.
  - A child with scaly patches and broken hairs.

  **Get urgent help** if you have low mood or thoughts of self-harm while taking finasteride or dutasteride. Stop the drug, then in the UK call 999 or NHS 111, and in the US call or text 988.

## 2. Figure out what kind of thinning you have

*This is a research summary, not medical advice. Only a clinician who examines you can diagnose the cause.*

Most thinning is not dangerous. Pattern hair loss is the most common cause, and about half of people show it by age 50. Shedding after a trigger such as illness or childbirth is also common ([DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/hair-loss)). With both of these, the scalp usually looks normal and does not hurt. The warning signs are different and are listed at the end of this section. Two causes can also happen at once, for example shedding on top of pattern loss.

**Four quick checks before you use the table** (you need good light and a phone camera):
- **Look at a hair you have shed.** A tiny white bulb at one end means it came out from the root. A short piece with no bulb means it broke off ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/shedding)).
- **Roughly count how many hairs you lose.** Losing about 50 to 100 hairs a day is normal ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hair-loss/), [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/shedding)).
- **Zoom in on the skin in the thin spot.** Check whether you can still see tiny pores where hairs grow, or whether the skin is smooth and shiny with none.
- **Check your eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, body hair and nails** for bare spots or small dents.

### What a dermatologist will do

The cause is usually worked out at the first visit. The dermatologist talks with you, examines you, and does one or two quick tests in the room. Other tests are added only when the picture is unclear or a particular cause is suspected ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/diagnosis-treat), [German S3 guideline 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/)). Almost all of these steps are based on expert agreement, not clinical trials. No randomised trials have compared different ways of diagnosing hair loss.

A trichologist is not a medical doctor and cannot take biopsies or prescribe medicines. For patchy loss, scarring signs or hair loss that does not fit a simple pattern, a dermatologist (ideally one who specialises in hair) is the right person to see ([BAD 2025 guideline](https://academic.oup.com/bjd/article/194/2/e56/8322888)).

**Steps at almost every visit**

1. **Questions about your history.** Expect questions on:
   - when it started, how fast it is going, and whether it was sudden or gradual
   - whether whole hairs are falling out from the root or breaking off
   - possible triggers 2 to 6 months earlier, such as childbirth, fever or illness, surgery, a crash diet or a big weight loss, stopping the pill, or new medicines like blood thinners, anti-seizure drugs or retinoids
   - scalp symptoms such as itching, burning, pain or pus
   - your hair care, such as tight styles and chemical treatments

   The German guideline gives these questions a strong recommendation. It gives a weaker recommendation to asking about major life events ([S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/), [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/shedding)).
2. **Examination.** The doctor looks at your scalp, eyebrows, eyelashes, beard, body hair and nails, and notes the pattern and extent of the loss. They also look for signs of scarring (missing follicle openings, smooth shiny skin), scale, redness, bumps, pustules or crusts. The main question is whether the loss is **scarring** (permanent) or **non-scarring**, and the AAD says the exam "provides vital clues" ([S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/), [AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/diagnosis-treat)).
3. **Pull test.** The doctor holds a tuft of 50 to 60 hairs close to the scalp and pulls gently. They do this at the edge of a patch and on the opposite, unaffected side.
   - **Normal:** fewer than 2 hairs come out.
   - **Active hair loss:** 10% or more of the hairs come out.

   In a study of 181 people, the average was under half a hair per pull, and 97% of people lost 2 or fewer. Washing or brushing beforehand made no difference in that study ([McDonald 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28010890/), [S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/)). It may cause slight discomfort.
4. **Trichoscopy (a scalp magnifier).** The doctor looks at the scalp and hairs through a handheld dermatoscope. It is painless and gives an answer straight away. What it shows depends on the cause:
   - **Alopecia areata:** no single sign proves it. The five typical findings are yellow dots, black dots, broken hairs, short fine hairs and exclamation-mark hairs (a review of 39 studies and 3,204 patients) ([Waśkiel-Burnat 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37357664/)).
   - **Scalp ringworm:** typical signs can justify starting treatment before culture results come back ([review 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31907867/)).
   - **Scarring alopecia:** scale around individual hairs suggests active lichen planopilaris, and red dots point mainly to discoid lupus ([DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/trichoscopy)).

   The German guideline recommends trichoscopy both to diagnose alopecia areata and to monitor it ([S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/)). Most of the research behind it comes from observational studies, so the percentages in it describe how often a sign is seen, not how accurate the test is.
5. **Standard photos and a severity score.** Photos are taken from fixed views, such as the whole head, the hairline and close-ups, so changes can be compared over time ([UK photo protocol 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39152787/)). For alopecia areata, the German guideline recommends recording a severity score called SALT at the first visit and at follow-ups, ideally with photos ([S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/)). Hair grows only about 1 cm a month, so photos usually need to be 3 to 6 months apart to show a real difference.

**What to bring to the appointment**
- **Photos:** recent ones of the top of your crown, centre part, front hairline, both temples and close-ups of any patch. Old photos from 1 to 3 years ago also help the doctor judge change ([S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/)).
- **A few shed hairs** in a labelled envelope.
- **A timeline:** when the loss started, and anything that happened 2 to 6 months before it, such as illness, fever, surgery, childbirth, a diet, stress, or stopping the pill.
- **A list of every medicine, hormone and supplement** with start dates and dose changes. Include the pill or hormonal IUD, HRT, testosterone and biotin.
- **Your hair routine:** how often you wash, relaxers or bleach, braids, weaves or extensions (how tight and how long you keep them in), and heat.
- **Symptoms:** scalp itching, burning or pain, plus any body-wide symptoms such as tiredness, weight change, heavy or irregular periods, joint pain, rashes or mouth ulcers.
- **Family history** of baldness, thyroid disease or autoimmune disease.
- **If you pull your hair,** your 1 to 2 week pulling log.
- **Come with clean hair and no cover-ups.** Wash out fibres, powders and sprays so the doctor can see your scalp.

### See a doctor soon if

**Get urgent help now if:**
- You have **thoughts of self-harm or suicide**, or new low mood while taking finasteride or dutasteride. Stop the drug and get help: in the UK call 999 or NHS 111; in the US call or text 988.
- You **chew or swallow hair** and have stomach pain, nausea, vomiting or weight loss. A hairball can block the gut.
- A woman shows **signs of a rapid hormone change**, such as a deepening voice or a fast increase in body hair, together with hair loss. One cause to check is accidental contact with a partner's testosterone gel.

**Book a dermatologist promptly if:**
- **Pain, burning, tenderness, itching, redness, pus, crusts or scale** are in the area of hair loss. This may be an infection, or a scarring alopecia in which lost follicles cannot regrow ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/diagnosis-treat), [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/hair-loss)).
- A bald area has turned **smooth and shiny with no visible pores**. This is a sign of scarring, and early treatment can stop it spreading. Ask about a biopsy.
- A **thin patch at the crown is slowly getting bigger**, especially in Black women. This may be CCCA, and the AAD advises booking an appointment now because delay risks permanent loss.
- The **hairline is moving back in a band** with eyebrow loss, especially after menopause. This may be frontal fibrosing alopecia.
- **Patches appear within days, spread, multiply or join up**, or you lose eyebrows, eyelashes, beard or body hair, or all your scalp hair. The British guideline lists prominent eyelash or nail involvement, and patches in other visible places, as reasons to see a specialist for suspected alopecia areata ([BAD 2025](https://academic.oup.com/bjd/article/194/2/e56/8322888)). Severe alopecia areata now has licensed treatments, so early specialist care matters.
- **A child has scaly patches with broken hairs**, or a soft, swollen lump with pus, with or without swollen glands. This may be scalp ringworm. It needs prescription tablets soon to prevent permanent scarring, and other people in the household may need checking ([DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/tinea-capitis)).

**See your GP (or the clinic named) if:**
- Hair loss comes with **tiredness, feeling cold or hot, weight change, palpitations, heavy periods or a swelling in the neck**. Get blood tests for thyroid and iron.
- **Iron deficiency** is found in a man or in a woman after menopause, or you have blood in your stool or a change in bowel habit. It needs checking for bleeding in the gut, including colon cancer.
- You have **joint pain, a rash on the face brought on by sun, mouth ulcers or fevers**. This may be lupus.
- You have **moth-eaten patches** with a rash on the palms or soles, sores or swollen glands. Get a syphilis test at a sexual health clinic.
- A woman has **acne, irregular periods or new facial or body hair** together with thinning.
- **Shedding lasts more than about 6 months**, or your hair is not back to normal one year after giving birth.
- **Hair loss started after a new medicine.** Talk to the prescriber, and do not stop blood thinners, beta blockers, anti-seizure drugs or other essential medicines on your own.
- **A child has hair loss** together with poor growth, tiredness, or puberty that is early or late, or a bald patch that has been there since birth.
- **The hair loss is causing real distress**, low mood, or avoidance of school, work or friends. Ask about counselling and support.


## 3. Causes, one by one

### Pattern thinning

*This is a research summary, not medical advice. "(unconfirmed)" marks claims the fact-check could not verify. Where the fact-check corrected a claim, the corrected version is used.*

#### Male pattern hair loss (androgenetic alopecia in men)

**What the spots look like**
It is not round bald patches. The thinning follows a pattern:
1. The temples recede, which gives an M-shaped front hairline.
2. A thin or see-through area appears at the crown (the back of the top of the head).
3. Hair thins evenly across the top of the head.

Over time the temple and crown areas can join up. In the thinning areas, single hairs become finer, shorter and lighter. This is called "miniaturisation". The skin of the scalp looks normal.

**Who gets it**
- It is the most common cause of hair loss. The European [EDF S3 guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29178529/) says the first signs usually start in the teenage years, and that it affects up to 80% of white men as they age.
- A [2025 Canadian consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40986632/) puts it at 40-50% of Canadian men and women by age 50.
- The [Japanese Dermatological Association (JDA) guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863806/) describes it as starting after puberty and getting worse slowly.
- It runs in families and is driven by male hormones. An enzyme (5-alpha-reductase) turns testosterone into DHT, and DHT gradually shrinks hair follicles in people whose genes make them sensitive to it ([Propecia label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=6f904709-65aa-44ce-b144-b4c8a0416e36); [review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42290527/)).
- [Norwood's 1975 study of 1,000 white men](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1188424/) found that baldness keeps getting worse with age, even in later years.
- Some studies link early male pattern hair loss with heart and metabolic problems:
  - A [Finnish case-control study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11455846/) linked it to coronary heart disease. Men with early hair loss had about 3 times the odds of needing a procedure to reopen heart arteries before age 60.
  - A [study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41312577/) linked it to metabolic syndrome. Only the title of this study was seen.
  - These are associations, not proof that one causes the other.

**How it's diagnosed**
- In most men, a doctor's questions and a look at the scalp are enough. They look for slow onset, a family history and the typical pattern.
- A 2024 systematic review of 34 studies says trichoscopy is mainly needed in women or in unclear cases. Trichoscopy means looking at the scalp through a magnifying skin scope (dermatoscope). The most common sign it shows is hairs of varied thickness.
- Sources for this section: [EDF S3 guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29178529/), [PubMed 21980982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21980982/), [JDA 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863806/).

**See a doctor if**
- **Round or patchy bald spots appear suddenly.** This is especially true with "exclamation-mark" hairs (short broken hairs that taper at the root) or loss of eyebrows, eyelashes or beard. It may be alopecia areata, not pattern loss. The minoxidil label says not to use it for sudden or patchy loss.
- **The scalp is red, scaly, itchy, painful, crusted or has pus spots, or the glands are swollen.** It may be a fungal infection, an infected follicle or an inflammatory disease.
- **The skin is shiny and smooth with no visible follicle openings, or the front hairline recedes in a band and the eyebrows thin.** It may be scarring hair loss. This needs a skin specialist quickly, because follicles lost this way do not come back.
- **Heavy shedding all over comes with other symptoms or a recent trigger.** Symptoms include tiredness, weight change, feeling cold or hot, a pounding heart, rash, mouth ulcers or joint pain. Triggers include a recent severe illness, surgery, fever or new medicine. Ask about telogen effluvium (temporary shedding), thyroid disease, low iron, lupus or a drug cause.
- **A child or young teen is losing hair, or loss is fast in the teens.** See a doctor before using any product. Over-the-counter minoxidil is labelled for age 18 and over.
- **You are on finasteride or dutasteride and have low mood, anxiety or suicidal thoughts.** Stop the drug and get medical advice urgently ([EMA 2025](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/news/meeting-highlights-pharmacovigilance-risk-assessment-committee-prac-5-8-may-2025)). Also see a doctor for sexual problems that do not go away, or for breast lumps, pain or nipple discharge.

**Check yourself**
- **Photo diary.** Every 2-3 months, photograph your hairline (front and both temples) and your crown (from above, with a phone held overhead or using a mirror). Use the same lighting and the same hair length, with hair wet and combed. True pattern loss keeps progressing. A hairline that is simply maturing moves back once, evenly, then stays put.
- **Pattern.** Thinning at the temples (M shape) and/or the crown or top, with full sides and back, fits pattern loss. Round, smooth, fully bald patches, or loss of eyebrows or beard, do not fit. See a doctor.
- **Hair quality.** Compare hairs from the temples or crown with hairs from the back of your head. Finer, shorter, lighter hairs in the thin area suggest miniaturisation. Hair of even thickness behind a slightly higher hairline suggests normal maturing.
- **Family history.** Pattern baldness in parents, grandparents or brothers makes it more likely. The minoxidil label says not to treat yourself if there is no family history or the cause is unknown.
- **Speed.** Pattern loss happens slowly, over years. Sudden heavy shedding (handfuls, or a much thinner ponytail or parting within weeks) points to another cause. Examples are temporary shedding after illness, stress, crash dieting or a new medicine.
- **Scalp skin.** Look for redness, scaling, pus spots, pain, itch or shiny scarred skin. Pattern loss leaves the skin looking normal.

#### Female pattern hair loss (androgenetic alopecia in women)

**What the spots look like**
It usually does not form real "spots". The hair slowly thins all over the top and crown, and the central parting gets wider. Follicles shrink step by step, so thick hairs turn into fine, short, wispy hairs. The front hairline may or may not stay in place ([Cochrane 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27225981/)). Doctors grade it on the Ludwig scale, from grade I (mild) to grade III (severe).

**Who gets it**
- It is the most common type of hair loss in women. The [EDF S3 guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29178529/) says it affects up to 42% of women and becomes more common with age. A 2025 meta-analysis cites up to 38%.
- It often runs in families ([NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hair-loss/)).
- It is linked both ways with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a hormone condition that can cause irregular periods, acne and extra facial hair. A [2025 review that pooled observational studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41035753/) found:
  - PCOS in 32.3% of women with female pattern hair loss (very low certainty)
  - pattern hair loss in 22.7% of women with PCOS (low certainty)
  - irregular periods in 20.7% of women with pattern hair loss.
- The review's authors advise that anyone diagnosed should be asked about period regularity, signs of high male hormones and fertility concerns.

**How it's diagnosed**
- Mostly by looking at the scalp. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hair-loss/) says a GP can often tell the cause by looking at the hair.
- Dermatologists often use trichoscopy, which means looking at the scalp through a magnifying skin scope. It shows hairs of mixed thickness and shrunken hairs.
- Because pattern loss is linked to PCOS, it is worth checking hormones before starting drugs that block male hormones (see the testing rows below).
- Sources: [Cochrane 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27225981/), [EDF S3 guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29178529/), [PCOS meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41035753/).

#### Alopecia areata (AA)

The main types are patchy AA, alopecia totalis (all scalp hair lost) and alopecia universalis (all scalp, face and body hair lost). Other forms are ophiasis (a band of loss along the hairline), diffuse AA and beard AA (alopecia barbae).

**What the spots look like.** Usually one or more smooth, round or oval bald patches about the size of a coin. The skin looks normal: no scarring, flaking or redness, and you can still see the tiny pores where hairs grow. Patches can get bigger or join up. A few people feel itching, tingling or burning, but this is uncommon. Regrowth often starts as fine, pale or white hairs. "Exclamation-mark" hairs are short broken stubs that are thicker at the tip and narrow towards the scalp. They are often found at the edge of a patch and point strongly to AA.

**Who gets it.** AA is an autoimmune disease. The immune system (specifically CD8+ NKG2D+ T cells) attacks hair follicles and stops them making hair, but does not destroy them. It affects all ages, sexes and ethnic groups.
- **How common:** about 1 in 1,000 people have it at any one time, and up to 1 in 50 get it at some point in their lives (Alopecia UK). A 2026 German S3 guideline puts the share of the population affected at 0.21% in 2020, with 72 new cases per 100,000 people that year.
- **Higher risk:** people with a family history of AA, a personal or family history of autoimmune disease, thyroid disease, vitiligo, atopy (eczema, asthma, hay fever) or Down syndrome.
- **Size of some risks (S3 guideline):** thyroid disease OR 1.91, vitiligo HR 3.13 and lupus HR 5.43. Both OR and HR compare risk with people who don't have the condition, so 1.91 means roughly 1.9 times the risk.
- **What doesn't seem to matter:** iron deficiency, pregnancy and vaccinations do not affect who develops AA. The evidence on vitamin D is conflicting.
- **Ethnicity:** the data conflict. The S3 guideline gives OR 1.77 for African-Americans and 0.40 for Asians compared with White people. A UK study cited by Alopecia UK found higher risk in Asian, Black and Mixed groups.

**How it's diagnosed.** A GP or dermatologist usually diagnoses it from how it looks. The 2026 German S3 guideline bases these steps on expert agreement:
- examine the whole scalp, the body and the nails
- look at every suspected case with a scalp magnifier (dermoscopy, also called trichoscopy)
- do a hair pull test at the edge of a patch

Up to half of people with AA have nail changes.

Sources: [Alopecia UK](https://www.alopecia.org.uk/alopecia-areata), [S3 guideline Part 1 (PubMed 41855094)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41855094/) ([full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC13340965/fullTextXML)), [BAD guideline 2025 (PubMed 41234147)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41234147/)

Ritlecitinib, deuruxolitinib and upadacitinib each have large placebo-controlled trials but no Cochrane rating yet, because the Cochrane search ended in July 2022. The other options rest on guidelines, smaller studies or expert agreement.

**Check yourself**
- Look closely at the patch in good light with a phone camera or magnifier. In AA the skin is smooth and normal or slightly peachy, with no flaking, crusts, pus spots or shiny scar-like skin. You can still see tiny pores.
- Look at the edge of the patch for exclamation-mark hairs: very short stubs that are thicker at the tip and narrow towards the scalp.
- Gently tug about 20-60 hairs at the edge of the patch. If several come out easily, the patch is probably still active.
- Check your fingernails for tiny pinpoint dents, ridges or roughness.
- Check your eyebrows, eyelashes, beard and body hair for other bare spots.
- Photograph the patches every 2-4 weeks with a ruler or coin for scale. Fine white or pale regrowth is a good sign.
- **See a doctor promptly if:**
  - the patch is red, scaly, crusted, full of pus, painful, or shiny and scarred with no pores. This could be scarring hair loss or an infection.
  - a child has scaly patches, broken hairs and swollen neck glands. This could be scalp ringworm.
  - patches spread fast over days to weeks, or you lose eyebrows, eyelashes or body hair.
  - you have all-over thinning plus tiredness, weight change, feeling too hot or cold, or a racing heart.
  - you feel significant distress, low mood or anxiety.
  - you are taking a JAK inhibitor and get a fever, a painful blistering rash, chest pain, a swollen leg, sudden breathlessness or new lumps, or become pregnant. Get urgent medical advice.

#### Traction alopecia

**What the spots look like.** Hair loss where hair is pulled hardest, most often the front hairline and temples. It can appear anywhere a style pulls, including under braids or extension attachments and in the beard area.
- **Early signs:** broken hairs around the forehead, a receding hairline, and thinning where hair is pulled tight. The scalp may show redness, itching, flaking or small pimples.
- **The "fringe sign":** a thin line of short, fine hairs often stays at the very edge, with a thinned band behind it.
- **Late stage:** smooth, shiny skin with no visible pores means scarring.

**Who gets it.** Anyone who often wears styles that pull, or puts repeated tension on the scalp.
- **Most often affected:** women and girls of African descent. A 2018 review says about one-third of women of African descent are affected.
- **Also at higher risk:** ballet dancers, military personnel, anyone who must tie their hair back for work, and Sikh men who twist uncut hair or beard tightly. Children can get it too, because styling habits often start young.
- **Cape Town studies:** found in 17.1% of African schoolgirls and 31.7% of adult women. Adults had higher odds than children (OR 1.87). Risk was highest when traction was added to chemically relaxed hair (OR 3.47 vs natural hair). It affected 48% of women who usually wore extensions on relaxed hair.
- **Other studies:** found it in 25% of 192 women in north Sudan and 76.3% of 207 women in Lagos.
- **What raises risk:** harder and longer pulling, and relaxers. The riskiest styles are tight buns or ponytails, weaves or extensions, and tight braids, cornrows and locs.

**How it's diagnosed.** A dermatologist asks about your hairstyling history and looks at the pattern, including the fringe sign. A scalp magnifier (trichoscopy) often shows hair casts, which point to pulling that is still going on, plus broken hairs, black dots, fine "baby" hairs and fewer hairs overall.

**Evidence level:** no guideline, no Cochrane review, no randomised trial, and no trials registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (searched 14 Nov 2026). The evidence comes from professional patient guidance, narrative reviews and studies that look at one point in time.

**Check yourself**
- **Pain test:** if a new style hurts, stings, gives you headaches or pulls the scalp up like a tent, it is too tight. Loosen it or take it out.
- **Monthly hairline check:** look in a mirror in good light, ideally taking photos from the same angle each month. Look for broken hairs, a receding edge or thinning at the front and temples.
- **Fringe sign:** a thin line of short, fine hairs at the very edge, with a thinned band behind it, suggests traction. A completely lost fringe together with eyebrow loss points away from traction.
- **Where is the thinning?** Traction loss matches where the style pulls. Thinning centred on the crown, sudden round bald patches, or all-over shedding suggest something else.
- **Feel and look at the skin.** Smooth, shiny skin with no pores suggests late, scarred disease. Broken hairs, redness or small bumps suggest the earlier, more reversible stage.
- **Review your habits:** braids or extensions left in longer than about 2-3 months, styles added to relaxed hair, glued weaves, daily tight ponytails or buns, tight headwear, rollers worn to bed.
- **See a doctor promptly if:**
  - you have shiny, smooth bald skin where hair used to be
  - you have pain, burning, pus, crusts, spreading redness or oozing
  - hair loss is spreading out from the crown, or the crown itches or burns. This could be CCCA, which is permanent if untreated.
  - your hairline is receding with eyebrow loss and no fringe of hairs left
  - you have sudden round bald patches, or lose eyebrows, eyelashes or body hair
  - hair keeps thinning even after months in loose styles, or the person with hair loss is a child

#### Scarring (cicatricial) alopecias: lichen planopilaris (LPP), frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA), central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA), discoid lupus (DLE) and folliculitis decalvans (FD)

**What the spots look like.** In all of these, inflammation destroys hair follicles and scar tissue replaces them, so **the hair loss is permanent**. Bald areas look smooth, white or pale and shiny, with no visible pores.
- **LPP:** patches on the crown, sides or lower back of the head, with redness and flaking around hairs at the edges. Those hairs pull out easily. Itching and pain are common.
- **FFA:** a band-like receding front hairline, often with eyebrow loss. According to the AAD, 80-95% of women with FFA lose eyebrow hair. Other signs are a paler, smoother strip of forehead skin and a few "lonely" hairs left in front.
- **CCCA:** starts at the crown and spreads outwards.
- **DLE:** red, scaly patches that leave scars or colour changes.
- **FD:** pus spots, crusts, a soft "boggy" feel, and tufts of several hairs growing from one spot.

**Who gets it.**
- **LPP:** usually adults aged 40-60. It is 2-5 times more common in women (BAD) and may come with lichen planus of the skin, mouth or nails.
- **FFA:** classically white women after menopause, but it also affects younger women, men and all ethnic groups. Black women tend to develop it younger. It is reported to be increasing.
- **CCCA:** the most common scarring alopecia in Black women, mostly in middle age. Variants of the PADI3 gene have been linked to it. Relaxers, hot combs and tight styles are suspected causes, but studies are inconsistent.
- **DLE:** more common in women in their 20s-40s and in people with darker skin. Smoking and UV light make it worse. A minority of patients have or develop lupus affecting the whole body.
- **FD:** mostly men in their 30s-50s; in one review the average age was 38 and men outnumbered women 3.3 to 1. It is thought to be an abnormal immune reaction to the bacterium *Staphylococcus aureus*.

**How it's diagnosed.** A dermatologist examines the scalp with a magnifier (trichoscopy). In LPP and FFA this shows missing pores, white dots, tube-shaped scale around hairs and redness. A scalp punch biopsy under local anaesthetic confirms the diagnosis. It should come from the active, inflamed edge of a patch, not the scarred centre. The BAD says at least 2 small areas are usually sampled, which leaves small scars.

**Evidence level:** no formal graded clinical guideline exists for any of these conditions. The highest-level documents are:
- an EADV task-force position statement on LPP (2026), built on a literature review and expert agreement
- a US expert consensus on CCCA (2024)
- a Cochrane review for DLE (2017)

- **Notice symptoms:** ongoing itching, burning, tenderness or pain on the patch are common in LPP, FFA and CCCA and unusual in ordinary pattern hair loss.
- **Hairline:** look for a paler, smoother band of forehead skin, a few "lonely" hairs left in front, or thinning eyebrows. These suggest FFA.
- **Other hair:** check eyebrows, sideburns or beard, armpits and limbs for new loss.

- **Signs of DLE or lupus:** check your face and ears for red, scaly or scarred patches. Note any joint pain or mouth ulcers.
- **See a doctor promptly if:**
  - you have smooth, shiny bald skin with no pores. Ask for referral and a biopsy before more follicles are lost.
  - the edge of a patch itches, burns or hurts and is red or scaly
  - your front hairline is receding in a band, especially with eyebrow loss
  - patches spread quickly or new ones appear within weeks to months
  - you have pus spots, crusts, a boggy feel or tufted hairs. Scalp fungal infection should also be ruled out.
  - you have red, scaly, scarring patches together with joint pain, tiredness, mouth ulcers or urine changes. Ask to be checked for lupus.

#### Tinea capitis (scalp ringworm, a fungal infection)

**What the spots look like.** Patches of hair loss with flaking skin. Some forms are more inflamed:
- **kerion:** a soft, swollen lump that may contain pus
- pus spots
- **favus:** crusts

Inflamed forms carry a higher risk of permanent, scarring hair loss. Hairs broken off at the scalp ("black dots") and grey, scaly patches are textbook descriptions that could not be checked against a source (unconfirmed).

**Who gets it.** Mainly children; the Cochrane review covers children only. People who carry the fungus on the scalp without symptoms may pass it on or reinfect the patient, which may explain some treatment failures (unconfirmed). It is commonly caused by *Trichophyton* fungi (such as *T. tonsurans*) or *Microsporum* fungi (such as *M. canis*). Which one is present changes which medicine works best.

**How it's diagnosed.** A clinician confirms the infection and ideally identifies the fungus, commonly with a lab culture of plucked hairs or scrapings. Other usual tools are microscopy of scrapings and a Wood's (UV) lamp. A Wood's lamp only picks up some infections, and the checker flagged the exact wording of that limit as needing correction; the full correction was not in the material.

**Check yourself**
- **Look at the patch.** Is the skin flaky, red or crusted, with hairs broken off at or just above the surface? That points to a fungal infection rather than alopecia areata, where the skin usually looks smooth and normal.
- **Who has it?** Is the person a child, or does a child in the house have similar patches?
- **Other people and pets:** is anyone else in the house itchy or scaly on the scalp or body? Do you have a pet with patchy fur loss, especially a kitten or puppy? *Microsporum* often comes from cats and dogs.
- **Lumps and glands:** is there a soft, swollen, tender or pus-filled lump? Are there swollen glands in the neck?
- **Creams not working?** If an antifungal cream has been used for weeks with no improvement, see a clinician about tablets.
- **Stop it spreading:** don't share combs, brushes, hats or pillows, and clean or replace shared hair tools.
- **See a doctor promptly if:**
  - there is a boggy, painful, pus-filled swelling, especially with fever or swollen neck glands. It needs tablets, not draining.
  - patches are spreading, or several family members are affected
  - there are yellowish or cup-shaped crusts with a mousy smell (favus)
  - signs of liver trouble appear on terbinafine or griseofulvin: yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, pale stools, ongoing nausea, tiredness, or pain on the right side of the belly. Stop the drug and seek care.
  - a rash, hives, facial swelling or trouble breathing appears after starting terbinafine
  - the person is or might be pregnant. Griseofulvin must not be used.

#### Trichotillomania (hair-pulling disorder) and other self-induced hair loss

**What the spots look like.** Irregular, oddly shaped patches that are never completely bald. The hairs in the patch are broken at different lengths. In alopecia areata, by contrast, hairs break more evenly and exclamation-mark hairs appear. Patches often sit where the hand reaches easily, and people may hide the loss. Under a scalp magnifier, a 2014 study of 44 patients found broken hairs in 100%, a "V-sign" in 57% and coiled hairs in 39%.

**Who gets it.** Pulling usually starts just before or after puberty. Adult trials enrolled mostly women; one trial had 45 women and 5 men, with an average age of 34. It is grouped with obsessive-compulsive related conditions as a "body-focused repetitive behaviour". People often feel tension before pulling and relief afterwards. Some pull without noticing, while others are fully aware. The scalp, eyebrows and eyelashes are the usual sites, but any body hair can be pulled.

**How it's diagnosed.** Mainly from your history (pulling, urges, tension and relief) plus an examination. A dermatologist can use a scalp magnifier to tell it apart from alopecia areata. In small 2023 studies, broken hairs of different lengths favoured trichotillomania, while black dots plus broken hairs plus exclamation-mark hairs favoured alopecia areata.

- **Ask yourself honestly, or ask family:** do you twirl, tug or pull hair while reading, studying, using screens, driving or lying in bed? Do you feel tension before and relief after?
- **Where are the patches?** Are they on the side of your dominant hand or on the crown? Are your eyebrows or eyelashes thinner?
- **Keep a 1-2 week log** of when, where and how you pull, and what you were feeling. This is the first step of habit reversal and is useful to bring to a doctor or therapist.
- **Rule out other causes.** Flaking, itching, pus or swollen glands suggest ringworm. Tight styles suggest traction hair loss.
- **Chewing or swallowing hair:** if you do this, tell a doctor even if you feel fine.
- **Get help urgently or promptly if:**
  - you swallow hair and have stomach pain, nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite or weight loss. A hairball can block the gut; it is potentially life-threatening and usually needs removal. Get urgent care.
  - you have low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of self-harm. Contact a doctor or crisis line promptly.
  - a child has scaly, itchy, oozing or boggy patches
  - there is shiny, scarred skin, or pain, burning or redness
  - hair loss is rapid and widespread without pulling
  - a young child is pulling hair. They need gentle, non-blaming assessment by a GP or paediatrician; behavioural therapy is the main option.

### Shedding and body causes

*This is a summary of research, not medical advice. Check every change to a medicine or supplement with a doctor. "(unconfirmed)" marks claims the fact-check could not verify. Much of the material on telogen effluvium and hair breakage came from search-result summaries of the cited pages, not the full texts.*

#### Telogen effluvium (TE): sudden or long-lasting shedding

**What the spots look like.** TE does not cause true bald patches. It causes thinning all over the scalp. You see more hair on the pillow, in the shower and in the brush, and hair looks thinner overall. The thinning is sometimes most noticeable at the temples or on top. Shedding usually starts about 2-3 months after the trigger, and rarely as early as about 2 weeks. After COVID-19, shedding started on average 63-74 days after the infection ([post-COVID review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9877680)). Losing about 100 hairs a day is often quoted as normal, but this is only a rough figure.

**Who gets it.** TE can affect anyone after a physical or emotional shock to the body. Common triggers are:
- childbirth
- severe illness or injury, especially with a high fever
- major surgery
- emotional stress
- a new or changed medicine
- COVID-19

The British Association of Dermatologists (BAD) says no cause is found in about 1 in 3 people. "Chronic" TE means shedding that lasts more than 6 months, and it is reported mostly in women ([BAD page](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/condition/telogen-effluvium/), [BAD leaflet 2025](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Telogen-effluvium-PIL-Oct-2025.pdf), [ISHRS](https://ishrs.org/telogen-effluvium/)).

**How it's diagnosed.** A doctor mostly relies on your history (a trigger 2-3 months earlier) and an examination of the scalp. In the hair-pull test, the clinician holds a small bundle of hairs (usually about 40-60) and tugs gently. What counts as a positive result varies by source. One common rule is more than 10% of the held hairs coming out; other sources use a count of about 10-15 hairs. Blood tests check for thyroid problems and iron deficiency ([BAD page](https://www.skinhealthinfo.org.uk/condition/telogen-effluvium/)).

**Warning signs: see a GP or dermatologist if you have:**
- Shedding that lasts more than about 6 months. This could be chronic TE, an ongoing trigger, or pattern hair loss underneath.
- Clear bald patches, broken hairs, or loss of eyebrows, eyelashes or body hair. These suggest alopecia areata or another condition, not TE.
- Redness, scaling, pain, burning, itching, pus spots, or shiny scarred areas on the scalp. These need prompt dermatology review.
- A widening part, or thinning at the crown or temples, without heavy shedding. This suggests pattern hair loss, which does not recover on its own.
- Hair loss with signs of thyroid disease, anaemia (breathlessness, extreme tiredness), major weight loss, or an eating disorder.
- Hair loss after starting a new prescription. Talk to the prescriber rather than stopping the drug.

**Check yourself**
- **Count back 2-4 months** from when heavy shedding began (on average about 2-3 months, or about 2-2.5 after COVID). Look for a fever or illness, surgery, childbirth, a crash diet, major stress, or a new or changed medicine. A clear trigger in that window strongly suggests TE.
- **Check the pattern.** TE thins hair all over, and many shed hairs have a small white bulb at the root end. Smooth round bald patches, or thinning only along a widening part or at the crown, suggest a different condition.
- **Try a gentle home pull test.** Don't wash your hair for about 48 hours. Then hold roughly 50 hairs near the scalp and slide your fingers along them. One or two coming out is normal. Five or more suggests active shedding. This is a rough guide only, and washing beforehand gives false reassurance.
- **Keep a shedding diary for a few weeks.** Use rough counts or photos of the hair in your brush and shower.
- **Look for short new hairs** at the hairline and part after a few months. Seeing them suggests you are recovering.
- **Ask your GP for blood tests** if you also feel tired, often too cold or too hot, have had weight changes, or have heavy periods.

#### Hair loss from medical conditions: thyroid disease, low iron, vitamin D and zinc, syphilis and lupus

**What the spots look like.**
- **Shedding after a trigger** (illness, medicines, stress or a nutrition problem) thins the whole scalp rather than making clear bald spots.
- **Thyroid disease** can cause either all-over thinning or patchy loss ([NHS hair loss](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/hair-loss/)).
- **Secondary syphilis** classically causes "moth-eaten" hair loss: many small, ragged patches with blurry edges and no scarring.
- **Lupus** can cause all-over or patchy hair loss without scarring. Discoid lupus on the scalp is different: it scars ([Perales 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37598015/)).

**Who gets it.**
- **Low iron** is found most often in women before menopause, usually because of periods or pregnancy. In men and in women after menopause, it usually comes from bleeding in the gut or poor absorption, so the cause has to be found. Ferritin (the blood measure of iron stores) can read falsely high during infection or inflammation ([Trost 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16635664/)).
- **Women with hair loss and low iron:** a 2022 analysis of 36 studies (10,029 people) found that about 21% of women with non-scarring hair loss had ferritin at or below 10-15. Their ferritin was on average 18.5 lower than in women without hair loss ([Treister-Goltzman 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35415182/)).
- **Low vitamin D** was found far more often in people with alopecia areata, an autoimmune condition that causes patchy hair loss. The odds of deficiency were about 3.9 times higher across 14 studies and about 4.1 times higher across 19 studies ([Lee 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29633370/), [Liu 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32772238/)).
- **Thyroid:** the NHS lists "dry skin, dry hair or hair loss" as symptoms of an underactive thyroid, and "patchy hair loss or thinning" as a sign of an overactive thyroid ([NHS underactive thyroid](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/underactive-thyroid-hypothyroidism/symptoms/), [NHS overactive thyroid](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/overactive-thyroid-hyperthyroidism/symptoms/)).
- **Syphilis:** hair loss is uncommon, affecting about 4% of people with secondary syphilis ([Bi 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19951624/)).
- **Lupus:** in one Nigerian study, 48% of 75 lupus patients had non-scarring hair loss.

**How it's diagnosed.** A dermatologist takes a history, examines the scalp, nails and any other areas of hair loss, and does a gentle pull test. A blood test or scalp biopsy follows only if they suspect a disease, deficiency, hormone imbalance or infection ([AAD diagnosis](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/diagnosis-treat), [AAD alopecia areata](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/types/alopecia/treatment)). A blood test can confirm thyroid disease (NHS). Anyone with moth-eaten hair loss should be tested for syphilis ([JAMA Intern Med 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40788633/)). Biotin supplements can distort several blood tests.

**Warning signs:**
- Moth-eaten, patchy hair loss, especially with a rash on the palms or soles, sores on the genitals or in the mouth, or swollen glands. Get a syphilis test at a sexual health clinic.
- Hair loss with joint pain, a rash on the face or one brought on by sun, mouth ulcers, fever, or protein in the urine. See a GP or rheumatologist about possible lupus.
- Scarring, redness, scaling, pain or burning in bald areas. Scarring hair loss, including discoid lupus, can become permanent without prompt treatment.
- Low iron in a man or post-menopausal woman, or with blood in the stool or a change in bowel habit. This needs checking for gut bleeding, including bowel cancer.
- Palpitations, tremor, marked weight change or a swelling in the neck alongside hair loss. Get thyroid blood tests.
- Chest pain while taking biotin. Tell the emergency team, because some heart tests can read falsely low.

**Check yourself**
- **Shape of the loss.** Overall thinning points to shedding, thyroid or iron problems. Many small, ragged patches (including in the beard or eyebrows) point to syphilis or alopecia areata. Smooth, shiny or scarred bald patches need a dermatologist.
- **Think back 2-4 months** for illness, fever, surgery, childbirth, crash dieting or weight loss, new medicines, or major stress.
- **Thyroid symptoms.**
  - Underactive thyroid: tiredness, feeling cold, weight gain, constipation, dry skin.
  - Overactive thyroid: weight loss, palpitations, tremor, sweating, neck swelling.
- **Signs of low iron:** heavy periods, recent pregnancy, a vegetarian or vegan diet, blood donation, tiredness. In men or post-menopausal women, any sign of gut bleeding.
- **Signs of syphilis:** a new sexual partner or sex without a condom, a painless sore, a rash on the palms or soles, white patches in the mouth, swollen glands, a flu-like illness.
- **Signs of lupus:** joint pain, a facial rash brought on by sun, mouth ulcers, fevers, extreme tiredness.
- **Biotin.** If you take biotin (often in hair supplements), remember it can distort thyroid and some heart test results.

#### Thinning linked to medicines, hormones and childbirth (plus chemotherapy hair loss)

**What the spots look like.** This is mostly telogen effluvium. Hair thins all over the scalp, so you get less volume or a thinner ponytail rather than clean bald patches. You may suddenly see more hair in the shower drain, brush or pillow. Some people also have scalp tenderness or odd sensations (trichodynia) ([BAD leaflet](https://www.bad.org.uk/pils/telogen-effluvium/)). Shedding typically starts about 2-3 months after the trigger. In a Japanese survey, shedding after childbirth started at an average of 2.9 months, peaked at 5.1 months and ended at 8.1 months after delivery ([PMID 38323220](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38323220/)). Chemotherapy causes a different type of loss, called anagen effluvium.

**Who gets it.**
- **Anyone after a body-wide trigger.** Triggers include giving birth, stopping the contraceptive pill or another hormone treatment, big weight loss or extreme dieting, high fever, illness or surgery, and starting a new medicine. It affects all ages and both sexes, and it does not run in families. No cause is found in 1 in 3 people (BAD).
- **After childbirth.** The Japanese survey found shedding in 91.8% of 331 new mothers. Only 21% of those asked replied, so this may overstate it. Breastfeeding for longer and early (preterm) labour made shedding more likely.
- **On GLP-1 weight-loss drugs, women are affected much more than men:**
  - Wegovy 2.4 mg trials: 4% of women vs 0.9% of men (placebo: 2% vs 0).
  - Wegovy 7.2 mg: 8.4% of women vs 0.2% of men.
  - Zepbound trials: 7.1% of women vs 0.5% of men (placebo: 1.3% vs 0).
  - Being female and losing more than 10% of body weight are the strongest predictors ([PMID 42873315](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42873315/)).
- **Chemotherapy.** Hair loss affects about 65% of people having chemotherapy ([PMID 41269388](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41269388/)).

**How it's diagnosed.** Usually from the history and an examination. The doctor looks for a trigger about 3 months earlier and examines the scalp ([BAD leaflet](https://www.bad.org.uk/pils/telogen-effluvium/), [CCJM 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42823125/)). In a pull test, more hair than normal comes out. The test can be falsely negative if hair was washed within the previous 48 hours.

#### Hair breakage (damage to the hair strand), including trichorrhexis nodosa

**What the spots look like.** Breakage is different from true hair loss. In true hair loss, hairs fall out from the root or the follicle (the pocket in the skin that grows the hair) stops making hair. In breakage, the hair strand snaps. It shows up as patchy "thin" areas of short, uneven broken hairs, often where heat, chemicals or pulling are worst: the crown, the hairline, the ends, or along a part. Broken ends are blunt or frayed. Under magnification, trichorrhexis nodosa shows weak points where the strand's fibres split and fray. On trichoscopy (a magnified scalp exam) this looks like two brushes pushed into each other ([PMC8719967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8719967)).

**Who gets it.**
- **People with heavy styling habits.** Breakage affects anyone who often uses chemical treatments (relaxers, straighteners, perms, bleach or colour), heat (blow-dryers, flat irons, curling irons), rough brushing or tight styles (braids, ponytails). The acquired (not inborn) form of trichorrhexis nodosa is one of the most common hair-strand defects. It is caused by physical or chemical damage, usually from heavy styling.
- **People with Afro-textured hair.** A review advises clinicians to ask about relaxers (sodium hydroxide, guanidinium hydroxide, ammonium thioglycolate), heated irons and plastic combs when they find broken strands.
- **Rare medical causes.** A rare form is present from birth. Weak hair is also linked with argininosuccinic aciduria, trichothiodystrophy and Menkes disease ([Medscape](https://img.medscape.com/pi/iphone/medscapeapp/html/A1073664-business.html), [Mount Sinai](https://www.mountsinai.org/health-library/diseases-conditions/trichorrhexis-nodosa)). Netherton syndrome and an underactive thyroid also appear in lists of associated conditions (unconfirmed).

The evidence here comes from case reports, textbook chapters and narrative reviews. No guideline or study of how accurate the tests are was found.

**How it's diagnosed.** A dermatologist first works out whether hair is shedding from the root, breaking along the strand, or both.
- **Tug test:** the clinician holds a bundle of hair near the root and near the tip and pulls. Hairs snapping in the middle point to fragile hair.
- **Hair card test:** a card in a colour that contrasts with your hair is held behind the area to show short broken hairs.
- **Other tests:** a pull test, trichoscopy (a good tool in clinic; dry trichoscopy shows trichorrhexis nodosa better) and, if needed, looking at hairs under a microscope. Microscopy is the gold standard for trichorrhexis nodosa ([PMC8719967](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8719967), [NYU Langone](https://nyulangone.org/conditions/hair-loss/diagnosis), [American Hair Loss Association](https://www.americanhairloss.org/types-of-hair-loss/hair-shaft-defects/)).

**Warning signs:**
- The scalp in the thin area looks smooth, shiny or scarred, or has lost its visible follicle openings. This can mean scarring hair loss, such as central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia in people with textured hair, or traction alopecia (loss from long-term pulling). It needs prompt dermatologist care, because the loss can become permanent.
- Itching, burning, tenderness, scaling, pus spots or redness in the area. This suggests a scalp condition rather than simple breakage.
- Smooth, round, completely bald patches, or loss of eyebrows or eyelashes. This suggests alopecia areata.
- Large amounts of full-length hairs with bulbs coming out, especially sudden shedding all over. This is shedding from the root, not breakage.
- Fragile, breaking hair in a baby or child, especially with developmental, skin or other health problems. This needs specialist assessment.
- Breakage or thinning together with tiredness, weight change, feeling cold, or other signs of thyroid disease or general illness.

**Check yourself**
- **Look at the hairs you lose.** A full-length hair with a small white bulb at one end fell out from the root. A short piece with no bulb and a blunt or frayed end broke. Mostly short pieces without bulbs point to breakage. (This tip comes from AAD-attributed advice in search results.)
- **Count roughly.** Losing 50-100 hairs a day is normal (AAD). If the hairs you lose are mostly short and your hair feels dry or brittle, breakage is more likely than true loss.
- **Try a card test.** Hold a card in a colour that contrasts with your hair behind the thin area. Many short hairs of uneven length with blunt or frayed tips suggest breakage. Fine, tapered short hairs are more likely new growth.
- **Match the thin spot to your styling.** Breakage sits where you clamp the flat iron, pull a ponytail or bun, apply relaxer or bleach, or brush hardest. Pattern hair loss follows the crown or part. Alopecia areata makes smooth, completely bald patches.
- **Check the skin.** With breakage alone, the scalp in the thin area should look normal: no redness, flaking, pus spots, shiny smooth skin, or missing follicle openings.
- **Note recent triggers.** Think of a new relaxer, perm or bleach, more heat, or new tight braids or extensions. Also note any illness, childbirth, crash diet or new medicine 2-3 months earlier. Those cause true shedding (telogen effluvium), not breakage.

#### Drug safety, monitoring, pregnancy and breastfeeding

**What this covers.** This section is about the drugs themselves, not a type of hair loss. It sets out the safety facts and the monitoring advised for the main hair-loss drugs. Most of it comes from official drug labels (FDA/DailyMed) and two regulator actions: the EMA's May 2025 review of suicidal thoughts with finasteride and dutasteride, and the FDA's 22 April 2025 alert on compounded topical finasteride. The safety evidence for low-dose oral minoxidil is weaker. It comes mostly from studies with no comparison group, reviews of those studies and expert consensus. There are no long-term randomised trials.

**Checks before and during treatment.**
- **Finasteride or dutasteride.** Tell anyone ordering a PSA (prostate) blood test that you take one of these drugs. They lower PSA by about 50%, so a new baseline is set about 3 months after starting and a single PSA value is doubled to interpret it ([Avodart label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=960797b3-09b1-4d6a-8ece-8b5d918e93e4)).
- **Low-dose oral minoxidil (LDOM).** Check blood pressure and pulse before starting and after each dose increase. Weigh yourself weekly and look for swelling.
- **Spironolactone.** Have a potassium blood test within 1 week of starting or changing the dose, then regularly ([Aldactone label](https://dailymed.nlm.nih.gov/dailymed/lookup.cfm?setid=0fed2822-3a03-4b64-9857-c682fcd462bc)).
- **JAK inhibitors.** Before starting you need a TB test (repeated during treatment), hepatitis B and C screening and a full blood count, and your vaccinations should be brought up to date (no live vaccines). Lipids are also monitored.

**Check yourself**
- On finasteride or dutasteride, check your mood and sexual function each month: low mood, loss of interest, sex drive, erections.

**Get help promptly if**
- You have thoughts of self-harm or suicide, or new low mood, on finasteride or dutasteride. Stop the drug and get urgent help: in the UK call 999 or NHS 111; in the US call or text 988.

#### Children, teenagers and young men under 25

**What it looks like.** Most hair loss in children develops after birth rather than being present from birth. The usual causes are scalp ringworm (tinea capitis), alopecia areata, shedding after illness or stress (telogen effluvium), hair pulling (trichotillomania) and traction from tight styles ([AAP Pediatrics in Review](https://publications.aap.org/pediatricsinreview/article/41/11/570/35358/Hair-Loss), [Xu 2017 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5522886/)).
- **Scalp ringworm:** scaly patches, sometimes with broken hairs. Sometimes there is a boggy, swollen, pus-filled lump called a kerion.
- **Alopecia areata:** smooth, round, completely bald patches with no scale.
- **Hair pulling:** hairs broken off at different lengths in a patch that still has some hair. The pull test is negative.
- **Loose anagen syndrome:** usually a young child whose hair is thin, short or uneven, and pulls out painlessly. It is less rare than people assume. A series of 374 children seen for hair loss found it in about 10% ([Cantatore-Francis & Orlow 2009](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamadermatology/fullarticle/712233)).
- **Aplasia cutis congenita:** a rare condition in which skin is missing, usually on the scalp. It is suspected when a bald patch has been there since birth.
- **Male-pattern hair loss in teens:** can start in the teenage years. People whose loss starts early usually end up with the most thinning as adults (Cutis review "Androgenetic alopecia in adolescents", indexed in PubMed as Price, 2003).

**How it's diagnosed.** The first step is to tell scarring from non-scarring loss. Clinicians then use a hair pull test, a trichogram (plucked hairs examined under a microscope) and trichoscopy (a magnified view of the scalp). These often avoid the need for a biopsy. For loose anagen syndrome, the trichogram showed typical loose hairs in 32 of 33 children. A pull test bringing out more than 3 (often more than 10) loose hairs is described as typical (unconfirmed). **Check yourself**
- **Look closely at the patch.** Smooth bare skin suggests alopecia areata. Scaly, crusted or swollen skin, or black dots and broken hairs, suggests ringworm and needs a doctor.
- **Look at the hairs in the patch.** Hairs broken at different lengths in a patch that still has hair suggest pulling. Notice whether it happens during homework, screen time or at bedtime.
- **Think back to birth.** A patch there since birth suggests a congenital cause, so see a children's dermatologist.
- **Notice whether a gentle tug pulls hairs out painlessly in a young child.** That can point to loose anagen syndrome.
- **For teenage boys and young men, check the temples and crown.** Receding at the temples or thinning at the crown, with a family history, suggests early male-pattern loss. Take photos every 3 months from the same angle and in the same light.
- **Think back 2-3 months.** Widespread shedding 2-3 months after illness, fever, surgery, a crash diet or major stress suggests telogen effluvium.

**Get help promptly if**
- There is a boggy, painful, pus-filled scalp swelling (kerion), especially with swollen neck glands. It needs prompt antifungal tablets, and delay can cause scarring.
- The skin looks shiny with no hair openings, which suggests scarring and permanent loss.
- Patches spread fast, or all scalp, eyebrow or body hair is lost.
- Hair loss comes with poor growth, tiredness, weight change, or early or late puberty. A doctor may test for hormone or nutrition causes.
- A bald or scarred patch has been present since birth, especially a large one.
- There are suicidal thoughts, self-harm or social withdrawal linked to hair loss, or mood changes on finasteride.

#### Hormonal life stages and hormone use

**What it looks like.**
- **Around and after menopause:** loss of ovarian oestrogen leaves male hormones (androgens) relatively stronger, and hair density and thickness drop. Female pattern hair loss (FPHL), shedding (telogen effluvium) and frontal fibrosing alopecia (FFA) all become more common. A 2026 review of 40 studies found FFA and FPHL had the strongest links with menopause, with higher risk after early or surgical menopause ([2026 review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41331233/)).
- **What the patterns look like:** in pattern thinning, women get a widening central part and thinning over the top. Men, and trans men on testosterone, get a receding temple hairline and crown thinning. FFA is a slowly receding band along the front and sides of the hairline that leaves pale, smooth, shiny skin. Eyebrow loss is often the first sign: 80-95% of women with FFA have it. FFA is usually diagnosed 2-12 years after menopause starts, and can appear earlier, particularly in Black women (AAD).
- **Testosterone replacement:** the AndroGel label lists hair loss as a side effect.
- **Anabolic steroids or SARMs (unapproved muscle-building drugs):** in 100 male steroid users, reports of hair loss rose from 2% to 12% over a cycle of about 3.5 months.
- **Trans men on testosterone:** in a study of 37,826 patients, male-pattern loss was 2.5 times as common as in cisgender women, similar to cisgender men. Trans women on feminising hormones did not have a significantly higher rate than cisgender men.
- **Levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena):** the label lists hair loss in under 5% of users. A New Zealand monitoring study estimated 0.33%.

**How it's diagnosed.** A dermatologist examines the hairline, scalp and eyebrows and takes a full medicine and hormone history: HRT, your exact pill or IUD, testosterone, steroids or SARMs. If FFA or another scarring alopecia is suspected, a scalp biopsy is done, and sometimes a biopsy of a facial bump ([Maturitas 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40318238/), [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35796569/)).

**Check yourself**
- **Take monthly photos** of your part line, crown and front hairline in the same light, and compare them over 3-6 months.
- **Check your eyebrows.** New thinning of the outer or whole brow after menopause is often the first sign of FFA.
- **Notice hairline symptoms.** Itch, burning or pain along the hairline, or small pimple-like bumps on the face, are FFA symptoms.
- **List every hormone product and its start date:** HRT (with or without testosterone), your exact pill or IUD, testosterone, steroids or SARMs, and any partner's testosterone gel. Match the dates to when thinning began.
- **Look for other signs of excess androgen:** new acne, new facial or body hair, a deeper voice, irregular periods.
- **On finasteride or dutasteride,** make sure whoever checks your PSA knows.

**Get help promptly if**
- The hairline is receding with smooth, shiny skin, the eyebrows are thinning, or there is itch, burning or pain at the hairline. FFA scars permanently, and early treatment can stabilise it.
- A woman develops a deeper voice, clitoral enlargement, fast-increasing body hair or male-pattern balding. Check for accidental contact with a partner's testosterone gel.
- A child shows early puberty in a home where someone uses testosterone gel.
- Someone using SARMs or steroids has yellow skin or eyes, dark urine, chest pain or psychiatric symptoms.
- Pregnancy is possible while taking finasteride or dutasteride, including in trans men with a uterus.
- Shedding is sudden and heavy, or bald patches appear that don't fit a gradual pattern. Don't assume hormones are the cause.

#### Inflammatory scalp conditions, and hair loss on Black and other dark skin

**What it looks like.**
- **Central centrifugal cicatricial alopecia (CCCA):** the most common scarring hair loss in women of African ancestry ([NEJM 2019](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=30763140), [JID 2026](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=41242654)). In a survey of 529 African American women, 5.6% had extensive central hair loss ([Olsen 2011](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=21075478)). Men get it too. In a series of 17 men, 88% were Black, 47% had a family history of hair loss and 29% had a second scalp condition. It starts at the crown and spreads outward, and may itch, burn or feel tender, or cause no symptoms at all. Early on it looks like female pattern hair loss. Later, the follicle openings disappear for good.
- **Fibrosing alopecia in a pattern distribution (FAPD):** thinning in the usual pattern-baldness areas, plus inflammation around the follicles. It is often mistaken for plain pattern loss. In 188 published patients, 164 were women, with an average age of about 54.
- **Acne keloidalis nuchae (AKN):** firm bumps at the nape, which may bleed after haircuts. It mostly affects men of African descent after puberty and rarely starts after 55. In one surgical review, 87% of patients were African American men.
- **Dissecting cellulitis of the scalp:** painful, boggy, pus-filled lumps and draining tunnels.
- **Scalp psoriasis and seborrhoeic dermatitis:** psoriasis gives thick scaly plaques, which can look grey or dark on brown skin. Seborrhoeic dermatitis gives fine greasy flakes and affects 3-10% of people. Psoriasis can cause hair loss, and anti-TNF drugs can trigger psoriasis with hair loss.
- **Pressure alopecia:** a patch of loss after the head was held still for a long time during surgery, intensive care or coma.

**How it's diagnosed.** Diagnosis uses your history, an examination, trichoscopy (a magnified view of the scalp) and often a biopsy. Trichoscopy looks different on dark skin. A healthy Black scalp normally shows a honeycomb pigment pattern and scattered pinpoint white dots, and early inflammation does not look the way it does on light skin. CCCA is diagnosed on average about 5.7 years after symptoms start. No trials of minoxidil specifically in Afro-textured hair were found.

**Check yourself**
- **Check the crown monthly** with two mirrors or a phone photo, in the same light and with the same parting. Is a patch spreading outward?
- **Look at the skin in the patch.** Shiny or smooth skin with no visible pores suggests scarring.
- **Notice symptoms in the patch.** Itch, burning, tenderness or tightness in a thinning area suggests active inflammation, which can be treated before it scars.
- **Feel the nape.** Firm bumps or bleeding after haircuts suggest AKN.
- **Look at the flakes.** Thick scaly plaques suggest psoriasis. Fine greasy flakes suggest seborrhoeic dermatitis.
- **Write down your routine** to take to appointments: how often you wash, relaxers, braids or weaves (how tight and how long they stay in) and heat.

**Get help promptly if**
- You have painful, boggy, pus-filled lumps or draining tunnels on the scalp.
- A thinning patch is smooth or shiny, or it itches, burns or is tender. Ask for a dermatology referral and a biopsy rather than waiting.
- Your scalp is sore or ulcerated after surgery or intensive care.
- A child has a scaly rash with patchy loss. Scalp ringworm needs tablets, not shampoo alone (NHS).
- Loss is sudden or spreading, or comes with eyebrow or body hair loss, weight loss, fever or joint pain.
- You get a new scalp rash or hair loss after starting an anti-TNF drug.

**What this covers.** This section is about treatments being sold and how to get proven ones, not a type of hair loss. #### Normal shedding, realistic timelines and the psychological side

**What it looks like.**
- **Normal shedding:** about 50-100 hairs a day ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/insider/shedding), [DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/hair-shedding)). Some reviews put the upper limit at 100-150.
- **Excess shedding (telogen effluvium):** heavy, even shedding across the whole scalp, noticed 2-4 months (often about 3) after a trigger such as illness, fever, childbirth, crash dieting, surgery or major stress ([DermNet](https://dermnetnz.org/topics/telogen-effluvium)). The sudden form usually clears on its own. In some people it carries on for longer (chronic telogen effluvium).
- **Thinning with age:** hair density falls with age. The frontal hairline pattern seen in girls before puberty remains in only about 45% of women aged 80 or over (seen in search results only).
- **Body dysmorphic disorder (BDD):** BDD is a condition in which a person is preoccupied with a flaw in their appearance that others see as small or invisible. Weighted figures across studies are 11.3% of dermatology outpatients and 9.2% of cosmetic dermatology patients (Veale 2016). Single studies range from about 4.5% to 35%.
- **Mental health:** anxiety and depression are more common in people with alopecia areata.

**How it's diagnosed.** Mostly by examination. A dermatologist looks at the pattern of loss and does a gentle pull test, which brings out more hairs in telogen effluvium. Trichoscopy (a magnified view of the scalp) can help.

## 4. Fixes ranked by evidence

*This is a research summary, not medical advice. It is current as of 14 November 2026. Get a diagnosis first: the right fix depends on whether the cause is pattern hair loss, alopecia areata, scarring alopecia or temporary shedding.*

**How to read the ratings.** These ratings are a summary judgement of the material below.
- **Strong:** the treatment is approved or guideline-backed, with good-quality randomised trials.
- **Moderate:** there are solid trials or guideline support, but with real limits.
- **Weak:** the studies are small, low-quality or uncontrolled, or the results come only from the company that makes the product.
- **None:** there is no evidence of benefit, or experts advise against it.

**Key terms.**
- **"SALT ≤20"** means 80% or more of the scalp is covered with hair.
- **"hairs/cm²"** means hairs per square centimetre of scalp.
- **"Off-label"** means a doctor prescribes a licensed drug for a use it is not licensed for.

### 4a. Cosmetic camouflage

Camouflage does not slow hair loss or regrow hair. It can help self-esteem ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/tips)).

**How it works.** Most camouflage reduces the colour contrast between the hair and the paler scalp. That is why it works best where some hair remains: diffuse thinning, a widening part, or small patches. A bald or scarred spot can be hidden only if it is small (under about 3 cm) and surrounded by normal hair ([ISHRS](https://ishrs.org/patients/treatments-for-hair-loss/camouflage-treatments/)).

**How common it is.** In an Alopecia UK survey of 338 people, mostly with alopecia areata, 46% said wearing a wig had a positive effect on everyday life. No guideline, systematic review or trial has measured whether camouflage works. The evidence is expert guidance, case series and surveys.

The NHS, BAD and DermNet are also cited as recommending camouflage in general as a valid option (unconfirmed).

**Practical tips.**
- Patch-test any new fibre, powder, adhesive or tape on a small area first.
- If you use minoxidil, apply it to a clean scalp and let it dry completely before adding fibres.
- Wash off all camouflage before a dermatology appointment.
- Every 4–8 weeks, photograph your part and crown without camouflage to track whether treatment is working underneath ([ISHRS](https://ishrs.org/patients/treatments-for-hair-loss/camouflage-treatments/); [FDA](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-products/hair-dyes)).

### What doesn't work (or isn't proven)

- **Biotin, if you are not deficient.** There is no evidence it helps, and high doses can make blood tests wrong, including a falsely low heart-attack test ([Patel 2017](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=28879195); [FDA warning](https://cardiovascularbusiness.com/topics/clinical/acute-coronary-syndromes/fda-warns-biotin-interference-troponin-tests)).
- **"Hair supplements" and mega-doses when you are not deficient.** Too much selenium, vitamin A or vitamin E can cause hair loss. Supplements are not checked for safety or effectiveness before sale ([AAD tips](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/tips)).
- **Rosemary oil and caffeine products.** Both are "not recommended" in the 2025 Canadian consensus. Rosemary oil rests on one trial with no placebo group. Most caffeine trials are low or very low certainty ([Canadian consensus](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40986632); [Szendzielorz 2025](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=39997270)).
- **Pumpkin seed oil.** One small trial of a branded mix, which still needs repeating ([Cho 2014](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=24864154)).
- **Onion juice.** It has never been tested in pattern hair loss, and is listed among unfounded "natural" cures. For alopecia areata, it may delay steroid treatment that works ([Sharquie 2002](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=12126069); [Misinformation review](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=40176994)).
- **Scalp massage.** Very low evidence: 9 men with no comparison group, plus a self-report survey ([Koyama 2016](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=26904154)).
- **PRP for alopecia areata.** No guideline recommends it. Steroid injections did better head-to-head, and it should not be offered to children ([German S3 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42076948/)).
- **PRP for pattern loss.** It is not proven to the level of minoxidil or finasteride; the evidence is low certainty ([Cruciani 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34967722/)).
- **"FDA-cleared" laser devices.** Clearance is not proof that a device works, and no device is cleared for darker skin types V–VI ([Perez 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39404126/)).
- **Hair transplant during active alopecia areata or active scarring alopecia.** Expert reviewers consider it contraindicated: surgery can trigger a flare, and the transplanted hair can be lost ([True 2021](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC8719975/fullTextXML)).
- **Artificial-fibre (prosthetic) hair implants.** The Japanese 2017 guideline says they should not be performed ([JDA 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29863806/)). Other claims about polyamide fibre implants such as Biofibre are all (unconfirmed):
  - the FDA suspended the procedure in the US in 1983 (unconfirmed);
  - in one series of 44 patients, about 9% had inflammation or infection, and about 20% of the fibres fell out each year, so repeat sessions are needed (unconfirmed);
  - larger series come from authors linked to the technique and have no comparison groups (unconfirmed)
  ([Santiago 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17214677/); [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29484017/)).
- **Clascoterone, extended-release oral minoxidil and other pipeline drugs.** None is approved yet. Their results so far are company-reported or not yet available ([Expert Opin Pharmacother 2026](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&rettype=abstract&retmode=text&id=42290527)).
- **Camouflage as a cure.** Fibres, sprays, wigs and SMP hide hair loss but do not slow it or regrow hair. They must not delay a diagnosis, especially if the scalp is red, scaly, shiny or painful ([AAD](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/hair-loss/treatment/tips)).
